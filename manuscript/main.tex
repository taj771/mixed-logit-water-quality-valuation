\begin{flushleft}
\large{\textbf{Varying Status Quo Conditions in Stated Preference Surveys: Empirical Evidence for Water Quality}}
\end{flushleft}

\subsection*{Abstract}


This study examines how current conditions shape people’s preferences for public policies that improve environmental quality. 
We use a stated preference survey with experimentally varied status quo levels in a setting with a spatially explicit freshwater 
health score across a wide geographic area. We provide causal evidence that people have a higher willingness to pay when status 
quo water quality levels are worse. These findings highlight the importance of accurately describing the status quo condition 
in stated preference studies. This study also provides empirical insights into the monetized economic benefits of landscape-wide 
water quality improvements in Canada. We find that the public’s willingness to pay for water quality improvements varies by 
spatial scale and proximity to the policy site. Local improvements in home basins or sub-basins are estimated to have a higher 
willingness to pay than distant ones. Recreational users have substantially higher willingness to pay, underscoring the joint 
role of non-use and use values.


\subsection*{3.2. Introduction}

Monetized benefits of water quality improvements play a crucial role in shaping environmental policies, particularly as the degradation 
of freshwater ecosystems has led policy makers to prioritize their management \parencite{unwater2021, naidoo2005economic, keiser2019low}. 
These benefits underpin cost-benefit analyses by quantifying trade-offs and net economic value to society, incorporating 
both market and nonmarket ecosystem services \parencite{goldstein2012integrating, naidoo2005economic, arrow1996there}. 
Stated-preference (SP) surveys, which estimate people's willingness to pay (WTP) for environmental improvements, 
are a cornerstone of this valuation \parencite{johnston2017contemporary}. The SP literature is continuously evolving to address 
the critical nuances of policy relevance and the complexities of regulatory decision-making \autocite{johnston2017contemporary, vossler2023valuing, moore2023measuring,bateman2023perspectives}.


SP practitioners often face difficult decisions when describing status quo (SQ) conditions to respondents. In SP surveys, 
the SQ alternative provides a baseline description of current or future conditions and acts as a comparison for the policy alternatives.
In many contexts and particularly water quality SP surveys, there is often limited data for accurately defining the SQ water quality 
condition. For example, limited data may suggest that water quality is either ``poor'' or ``moderate'' in an area and the researcher needs to 
decide which water quality to use as a SQ in a SP survey.
Do these researcher decisions regarding the SQ description affect elicited preferences and values for environmental changes?


To study this question we employ a split-sample approach to isolate the effect of 
status quo conditions, thereby providing a robust assessment of how public preferences and welfare measures vary with the status quo 
presented to respondents. To do so, we conducted a SP survey administered to 4,000 households across the Canadian prairie provinces 
of Alberta, Saskatchewan, and Manitoba. Our approach builds on recent advancements in SP research, including improved identification of 
ecosystem services, refined water quality metrics, and the incorporation of spatial dimensions through interactive visual maps—all 
designed to align with regulatory objectives \autocite{moore2023measuring, kling2012exxon, johnston2017contemporary, vossler2023valuing, bateman2023perspectives}.


We employed a four-version split-sample experimental design, with each version incorporating a distinct, experimentally varied SQ 
water quality level. Respondents were randomly assigned to one of four survey versions, each featuring a unique SQ water quality 
level based on the World Wildlife Fund’s (WWF) freshwater health score, adapted for Canada \parencite{WWF2020}. While choice attributes 
and proposed improvements remained consistent across versions, the SQ was manipulated to vary respondents’ baseline perceptions. This 
design enables us to isolate the causal impact of SQ on the utility of water quality improvements, overcoming the challenges 
associated with relying solely on observational variability of SQ across river basins. In particular using observed SQ introduces endogeneity, 
as actual water quality is often correlated with unobserved factors, such as socioeconomic conditions, land use, pollution sources, or 
differential exposure, meaning people in poorer-quality areas may have different priorities or less experience with high-quality water. 
In contrast, experimentally controlled SQ allows us to isolate causal effects by randomly assigning baseline water quality levels, 
thereby mitigating the endogeneity and perceptual biases inherent in observational data. This experimental control provides a robust 
framework for identifying how SQ water quality levels affect elicited preferences and values for water quality changes.


We find that people's preferences for water quality improvements varies by spatial scale and proximity to the improvement.
The annual household marginal WTP for an improvement in the water quality score is estimated to be 44\% higher for 
local basins (\$226) than for non-local basins (\$156). For sub-basin-level improvements, marginal WTP is \$147 for local sub-basins and \$86 
for non-local sub-basins. These findings highlight the importance of spatial context in shaping public preferences for water quality 
interventions, informing more effective and equitable environmental policies in Canada.

Our results indicate that public preferences for water quality improvements vary systematically with the SQ water quality conditions. 
By leveraging experimental variation in SQ, we find robust empirical evidence that researchers’ decisions regarding how the SQ water 
quality level is framed meaningfully influence revealed preferences in SP valuation. Specifically, poorer SQ conditions amplify the 
utility associated with improvements, resulting in up to a 52\% higher willingness to pay for equivalent improvements when the baseline 
is severely degraded compared to when it is relatively healthy, particularly for improvements at the local sub-basin scale. These findings 
highlight the importance of accurate definition of baseline water quality in SP studies, as it systematically influences public preferences 
for water quality improvements and consequently affects policy-relevant valuations and environmental decision-making.


This study advances the SP literature on water quality valuation by systematically examining how SQ water quality conditions 
shape public preferences, an underexplored area in prior research. The limited number of studies that have explored this issue 
have focused on the changing nature of the SQ conditions and whether environmental quality is improving, remaining the same, or 
decreasing under the SQ. For instance, \textcite{maguire2018muddying} used a three-way split-sample design with valuation questions 
that incorporated a constant, a decline, or an improvement in future conditions without policy interventions across survey versions.
Their findings reveal that WTP varies with future SQ trajectories, with marginal WTP increasing from constant to declining to 
improving conditions, highlighting reference dependence in shaping policy support.  A related stream of the 
SP literature has elicited perceptions of SQ water quality and compared responses to researcher-defined SQ conditions, 
finding that the SQ-perceived group exhibited stronger preferences and higher WTP for water quality improvements \parencite{marsh2011respondents}. 
This underscores the critical influence of perceived SQ on environmental valuation outcomes, although self-reported baseline SQ 
risks endogeneity with preferences. In contrast, our study uses a split-sample design that exogenously manipulates current SQ water 
quality across respondents, avoiding reliance on future projections or subjective reports. This experimental control enables a more 
rigorous test of SQ effects, controlling for endogeneity concerns that arise from relying on observational variability of SQ. 
It also offers empirical insights into the role of SQ conditions in preference formation, with critical implications for reducing 
uncertainty in regulatory analysis and improving the design of environmental valuation surveys.

This study also contributes to the limited Canadian SP literature on valuing water quality, where only a few studies have addressed the 
complexities of regional and policy-relevant contexts \parencite{macaskill2022six}. Compared to the U.S., progress in Canada has been 
slower in aligning SP applications with regulatory decision-making needs. We advance this literature by employing a comprehensive 
water quality index that captures the interplay of human activities, pollution, and ecological integrity, producing more robust WTP 
estimates for realistic policy contexts \parencite{arrow1996there, carson1993value, moore2023measuring}. By integrating detailed water quality 
measures, spatial heterogeneity, and rigorous survey design, this study strengthens the accuracy and policy applicability of SP 
literature on water quality valuation in Canada.



\subsection*{3.3. Study Area}

In this study, we focus on four major river basins within the Prairie Provinces (Alberta, Saskatchewan, Manitoba), 
spanning over 800,000 square kilometers, which are further divided into 18 sub-basins (Figure~\ref{Figure1}). 
These watersheds are critical to agriculture and the economy, providing irrigation
for extensive farmland, potable water for urban centers, and essential ecosystem services such as water 
purification and flood mitigation \parencite{schindler2006impending}. Consequently, the region faces significant ecological 
threats, particularly from agricultural runoff, nutrient loading, and habitat fragmentation, which pose 
risks to water quality and ecosystem resilience. Climate change, land-use changes, and potential water 
scarcity further amplify the ecological and economic costs of degradation \parencite{WesternEconomicDiversificationCanada2020, 
schindler2006impending}. Empirical evidence on monetized economic benefits and ecological integrity 
remains a critical component of policy decision-making, given the region's economic dependence, complex 
transboundary nature, ecological vulnerabilities, and climate-driven pressures for managing water resources
sustainably \parencite{warren2022canada}.

\subsection*{3.4. Stated Preference Survey}

\subsubsection*{3.4.1. Survey Structure}


We built on \textcite{vossler2023valuing} and tailored the survey instrument to the Canadian context. In particular the survey included four 
distinct sections, and the full survey instrument is provided in the supplimnentary materials. The online survey encouraged respondents
to use a laptop, desktop, or large tablet for optimal viewing of graphics. This was followed by an authentication screen requesting the first 
three digits of the respondent’s residential postal code to verify their location and proceed with the survey.\footnote{Since only the first 
three postal code digits were collected, some Forward Sortation Areas (FSAs) spanned sub-basin borders. For those respondents, we included 
questions with a map showing sub-basin distribution across the province, asking them to select their local sub-basin.} Introductory screens 
outlined the study’s purpose, researcher affiliations, and government funding through the Global Water Futures program. 

In Section 1, respondents provided opinions on water resources and conditions near their homes. Section 2 used visual and written descriptions to characterize variations 
in water appearance, natural flow, and biodiversity. To verify image comprehension and act as attention checks, respondents selected the image 
depicting the best water quality from a set of three. Incorrect answers triggered a refresher on image interpretation. These images were paired 
with detailed variations corresponding to the WWF's five-level health score, with lower numerical values assigned to more naturally functioning 
ecosystems, as shown in Figure \ref{Figure2}. After reviewing water condition levels, respondents viewed a map showing the spatial distribution of water 
quality for a non-local basin and identified the water quality level of a specific city within it. Using a map of their local basin, they 
identified the water quality of their local basin. Section 3 included valuation scenarios, detailed in the next section. It concluded with 
follow-up questions assessing whether respondents’ votes aligned with incentive-compatible elicitation, which attributes influenced their 
choices, their response uncertainty, beliefs about the tax burden’s distributional aspects, and general thoughts on the water quality proposals. 
Section 4 was brief and primarily focused on water-based recreation, including detailed trip information for 2024.



\subsubsection*{3.4.2. Characterizing Water Quality and Status Quo for Respondents}

Water quality is complex to measure, and SP practitioners often face challenges in conveying water quality and the 
SQ to respondents. We use the overall freshwater health scores, derived from the WWF Canada Freshwater 
Assessment framework, to define water quality as a commodity for which we measure preferences \parencite{WWF2020}. 
This national-scale framework measures deviations in ecological integrity from a water body’s natural condition, 
provides a standard, scientifically grounded method for evaluating health across Canadian river basin and sub-basins 
based on the international best practices \parencite{boulton1999overview,Roux1999,Norris1999,Birk2012,USEPA2013} and 
domestic initiatives \parencite{Kidd2011,Alberta2012,Maaskant2012}. The overall health score is a composite 
index based on four core metrics: hydrology, water quality, benthic macroinvertebrates, 
and fish, which represent key components of aquatic ecosystems and are widely monitored in Canada \parencite{WWF2020}. 
Each metric is assessed through quantitative indicators that measure current status and, where sufficient data exist, 
temporal trends, with data sourced from long-term monitoring by federal, provincial, and regional entities, including 
Environment and Climate Change Canada, provincial governments, community groups, and Indigenous-led 
initiatives\footnote{More details on WWF's technical protocols for health assessment, the derivation of the overall 
water quality index, and the specific data sources are available in the Technical Protocol report at https://watershedreports.wwf.ca.}. 
We used the health scores published during the 2020 assessment, which were the most recent available at the time of survey development
\footnote{We also reviewed the most recent health scores published in 2025. When comparing the health scores used in our survey with the most 
recently published data, we found no significant changes between the two assessments. Most importantly, none of the 
missing health scores have been updated in the latest edition}.


The WWF's freshwater health score is attractive for our purposes, as it is designed to provide comparable interpretations of 
biological health across watersheds, offering spatial transferability and grounding in scientifically credible ecological 
principles. We use these spatially explicit health scores to carefully define water quality as an environmental good and 
service to be valued, ensure consistent interpretation across respondents, and generate empirical estimates that are broadly 
comparable across space and linked to a policy-relevant metric of ecological integrity \parencite{vossler2023valuing}. The 
overall health score is categorized into five levels: very poor, poor, fair, good, and very good, with each level reflecting a 
distinct degree of change from baseline ecosystem function and integrity at the sub-watershed scale. The scale of the scores 
captures the extent of impacts from various stressors resulting from human activities in watersheds. The status of water quality 
can therefore be improved by actions undertaken to address anthropogenic stressors, such as enhancing natural water flow and 
controlling nutrient loading from agricultural land use \parencite{chambers2012development,vossler2023valuing}.

We translated the ecological concepts underlying each health score into both visual and textual representations, ensuring they 
are understandable to survey respondents in order to elicit economic values associated with water quality improvements. 
In particular, we characterize the appearance of a natural stream by highlighting visual features such as water color and clarity, 
physical modifications that alter the stream’s natural flow, the condition of riparian zones and banks, and the diversity of fish, 
benthic macroinvertebrates, and aquatic plants supported by varying levels of health score. These are important drivers or are 
associated with the core metrics upon which the overall health score is built, and are therefore likely to be associated with 
different health scores and serve as key determinants of the ecosystem conditions of natural streams. We summarized this information 
along with the varying visual features corresponding to each of the five levels of the overall health score. Each level visually 
depicts the appearance of a natural stream or river, accompanied by a snapshot of biological diversity illustrated through a 
gradient of benthic macroinvertebrates and aquatic plants. The visual and associated verbal narratives corresponding to five level 
of health score define the water quality as commodity (Figure \ref{Figure2}).

To incorporate the spatial dimension of water quality improvements, we assigned spatially explicit baseline health score values across all 
sub-basins in our study area (Figure \ref{Figure1}). At the time of data acquisition, some sub-basins lacked health score values due to 
data gaps. For instance, among 18 sub-basins across four basins within the study area, eight sub-basins did not have an overall health 
score. To address this issue, we imputed health scores for sub-basins with missing data based on the overall health scores of 
neighboring sub-watersheds, their flow directions, and the threat levels assigned to the sub-watersheds with missing data.\footnote{We 
used the WWF Freshwater Assessment Report to identify threat levels at the sub-basin level. The framework incorporates key indicators to 
capture current stressors in Canada’s freshwater systems.} To account for the uncertainty associated with this imputation, we considered 
two possible scenarios of the assigned health scores: in one scenario, we assigned a higher health score, and in the other, we assigned a 
lower health score for the missing cases.

The overall health scores are based on the WWF Freshwater Assessment in 2021, which was the most recent health score available at the 
time of the study. Since we propose water quality improvements for a future time span, we adjusted the current baseline health scores based 
on assessed threat levels to account for potential recent and future changes in water quality. For these threat-based adjustments, we 
considered two baseline scenarios. In the first scenario, we downgraded the health score by two units if the threat level was assessed as 
high or very high, and by one unit if the threat level was assessed as moderate or low. In the second scenario, we downgraded the health 
score by one unit only if the threat level was assessed as very high; all other cases remained unchanged.

In total, this resulted in four potential baseline health score scenarios for each sub-basin. For sub-basins with missing overall health 
scores, variation stemmed from both the initial imputation of health scores and the threat-based adjustment. For sub-basins with available 
overall health scores, variation resulted solely from the two threat-level adjustment scenarios.\footnote{The threat-based adjustments 
were nested within the initial imputation of missing health score values, resulting in four baseline conditions.} Across the four baseline 
conditions, 5.6\% of the study area had a baseline health score classified as “very good”, 66.7\% had scores ranging from “good” to 
“fair”, 16.7\% had scores ranging from “fair” to “poor”, and 11.1\% had scores classified as “very poor”, based on the WWF freshwater health 
score classification, as shown in Figure \ref{baseline_healthscore_map}.

\subsubsection*{3.4.3. Valuation Questions}

The valuation exercise was designed to empirically estimate the degree to which people prefer ecosystems closer to their natural state. To 
estimate households’ WTP for improvements in water quality, the survey presented up to seven valuation scenarios tailored 
to each respondent. We followed established survey best practices to encourage truthful responses and to interpret answers to the valuation 
scenarios as reflections of real economic trade-offs. Drawing on the literature on incentive-compatible survey elicitation 
\parencite{carson2007incentive, vossler2012truth}, we framed the valuation scenarios as advisory referenda, employed a coercive payment 
mechanism, and instructed respondents to consider each referendum independently \parencite{vossler2023valuing}. Furthermore, we emphasized 
the survey’s consequentiality by informing participants that it was government-funded and that the results could influence public policy decisions. 


The survey included valuation scenarios defined by four attributes: the spatial scale (basin or sub-basin), whether the change occured in 
a local or non-local area, the water health score change, and a cost attribute (Table~\ref{Table 1}). We use experimental variation to examine 
how economic welfare responds to two spatial dimensions, based on four different levels of improvementin the health score. The first spatial 
dimension is the scale of water quality changes, where we propose improvements at either the full basin level or only within a sub-basin. 
The second spatial dimension is the location of the changes, which has two levels: whether the changes occur within the respondent’s local 
basin or not. The tax increase was defined as a household-level annual tax, randomly assigned and unavoidable if the referendum was approved, 
applied over five years period. To reduce respondent cognitive burden, we grouped the scenarios into spatial unit blocks and water health score 
change blocks. Within the spatial unit blocks, we defined two spatial scales: an entire river basin and sub-basin. Within a spatial unit block, 
we held the spatial scale fixed while varying the health score change scenario (four possibilities). In a health score change block, we held 
the health score change scenario fixed while varying the spatial unit. For each respondent, we assigned either two spatial unit blocks or two 
health score change blocks, with one block representing their local basin or sub-basin and the other representing a non-local basin or sub-basin.
We determined each respondent's local sub-basin based on their postal code provided at the beginning of the survey, which was then matched to 
both their local and non-local sub-basins. By presenting scenarios that either include or exclude the respondent’s local basin, we are able 
to differentiate the economic values of nearby improvements and how these values vary with distance.

\subsubsection*{3.4.4. Survey Testing}

We used the Voxco survey engine to code the survey for completion by an online panel. The experimental design and survey functionality 
were tested in July 2024 through two online focus groups, each with 8–9 participants, to ensure effective operation and clear materials and 
questions. Participants lived in the study area, were recruited using random digit dialing, and were invited to attend one of the potential dates.
Drawing on focus group insights, we conducted a pilot survey with 1,000 respondents across three provinces in December 2024 using the revised 
survey instrument to verify functionality and gather preliminary results, which informed further refinements to the final survey. Additionally, 
the pilot survey data were used to estimate preliminary models, confirming that our experimental design worked. Based on these insights, 
we adjusted the bid levels while keeping the rest of the experimental design unchanged.

\subsubsection*{3.4.5. Survey Administration}

The final survey instrument was administered to a sample of 3,850 people residing in our study area, verified by postal codes, between July 
2025 and August 2025\footnote{Though our data collection spanned three months, the majority of responses were collected during the early 
phase of the online survey. A growing literature examines whether public preferences are sensitive to temporal factors such as seasonality 
and survey timing. Assessing these temporal dynamics represents a potential extension of our research.}. %The survey instrument was 
%reviewed and approved by the Human Research Ethics Office of the University of Saskatchewan.% 
The survey was conducted in partnership with the Canadian Hub for Applied and Social Research (CHASR) using CHASR’s online probability-based 
panel. We used an opt-in internet panel to survey the general population in the Prairie provinces living in the study area, using quota-based 
sampling. With opt-in internet panels, participants may be more likely to complete a survey if they are interested in the topic, raising 
concerns about non-response bias (Johnston et al., 2017). To reduce potential self-selection bias, the survey invitation email omitted information 
about the survey topic. A quota-based sampling strategy ensured the sample was representative of province of residence, age, and gender. 
Furthermore, we used appropriate weighting for respondents to align with the 2021 census population targets. %(see Figures in A3.1-A3-12 in the Supplementary Materials).


\subsection*{3.5. Econometric Framework}

The theoretical framework for the econometric analysis of data from our discrete choice experiment is Random Utility Maximization (RUM) 
theory, which assumes that the utility an individual derives depends on both the observable attributes of the choices and unobserved 
factors affecting well-being, the latter captured through a random component (McFadden 1974). Let the utility of respondent $i$ derived 
from option $j$ in voting scenario $t$ be given by:

\begin{equation}
U_{ijt} = \beta_{program} + \alpha C_{ijt} + \beta^{sl}_{wq} \triangle WQ^{sl}_{ijt} + \beta' X_{ijt} + e_{ijt}
\label{Eq1}
\end{equation}

\noindent where utility $U_{ijt}$ is the utility that individual $i$ derives from alternative $j$ in choice situation $t$, and is assumed 
to be additively separable into the program-specific constant for the water quality improvement program $\beta_{program}$, cost $C_{ijt}$ 
(presented to respondents as an increase in household taxes), water quality changes for spatial unit $ sl $, 
with $ s \in\ \{ \text{Basin, Sub-basin} \} $ and $ l \in \{ \text{Local, Non-local} \} $ and a vector of other 
attributes $X_{ijt}$. The vector $X_{ijt}$ includes individual-specific covariates invariant across alternatives $j$ and thus cancels 
in the utility difference, not affecting choice probabilities. $\alpha$, $\beta^{sl}_{wq}$ and $\beta$ are the estimable parameters 
and $e_{ijt}$ is a random error term assumed to be independently and identically distributed Type I extreme value.

First, we estimate Equation~\ref{Eq1} using a binary logit (BL) model specification \parencite{train2009discrete}. Second, we use 
random parameter logit (RPL) models that allows utility parameters to be random and follow parametric distributions \parencite{revelt1998mixed}. 
The RPL approach accommodates multiple sources of unobserved variability, including heterogeneity in tastes across respondents, and it also 
accounts for panel data from repeated choice tasks. In particular, we model preferences for water quality improvements across varying spatial 
units and the program-specific constants as random, using a normal probability density function, the most common specification for 
characterizing preference heterogeneity \parencite{train2009discrete}.\footnote{All models were estimated using the Apollo package in 
R \parencite{hess2019apollo}. To estimate the random parameter logit (RPL) models, we used simulated maximum likelihood estimation with 5,000 Sobol draws.}

\subsubsection*{3.5.1. Welfare Measures}

Within this estimation framework, the negative of the parameter on the cost variable, $-\alpha$, measures the marginal utility of income. 
The parameter on the water quality score, $\beta^{sl}_{\text{wq}}$, represents the marginal utility of a change in water quality at a given 
spatial scale ($sl$). Since an increase in the water quality score corresponds to a decline in water quality, the estimated 
$\beta^{sl}_{\text{wq}}$ is negative, reflecting the disutility of worsening water quality. 
Consequently, the negative of this parameter, $-\beta^{sl}_{\text{wq}}$, measures the marginal utility of a one-unit improvement in water 
quality (i.e., a one-unit decrease in the score). Dividing the marginal utility of a one-unit decrease in the water quality score by the 
marginal utility of income yields the marginal WTP for a one-unit improvement in water quality at different spatial 
scales, as follows:


\begin{equation}
    WTP^{sl}_{\text{marginal}} = \frac{-\beta^{sl}_{\text{wq}}}{-\alpha}
\end{equation}

\noindent The total WTP ($WTP^{sl}_{total}$) measures the monetary value of a specific water quality improvement scenario compared to 
the current condition. This is calculated by dividing the difference in utility between the improved and current scenarios by the marginal utility of income. 
The utility difference accounts for the change in the water quality score and the program-specific constant ($\beta_{{program}}$), 
which captures the baseline utility of the improved scenario relative to the status quo. The 
total WTP is given by:

\begin{equation}
    WTP^{sl}_{total} = -\frac{\beta_{program}+(WQ^{sl}_{improved}-WQ^{sl}_{current})*\beta^{sl}_{wq}}{\alpha}
\end{equation}

\noindent We apply the delta method to calculate standard errors for the welfare measures, ensuring robust inference for the WTP estimates derived 
from Model 2 \parencite{train2009discrete}.


\subsection*{3.6. Results}

The results section is organized into four subsections. First, we present findings from the primary models estimating preferences for 
water quality improvements and discuss the associated welfare estimates.  Second, we extend the modeling framework to explore the 
effect of status quo water quality conditions on shaping public preferences for water quality enhancements. Then, we examine model 
specifications that evaluate how spatial attributes and respondents’ recreational behavior influence public preferences for water 
quality improvement programs. Finally, we assessed survey response validity to ensure reliable data and conducted sensitivity analyses to 
evaluate result robustness.

\subsubsection*{3.6.2. Impact of Status Quo Water Quality Conditions}

Table \ref{baseline} summarizes the estimation results from the model examining how SQ water quality conditions influence public preferences 
for water quality improvement programs. In our experimental design, variation in SQ water quality arises not only from inherent differences 
across sub-basins but also from experimentally induced variation across survey versions within those sub-basins. Our objective is to assess 
whether this exogenous, experimentally assigned variation in SQ affects preferences for water quality improvements, thereby addressing the 
endogeneity concerns that arise when relying solely on observational differences in SQ across locations. To do so, we evaluate in Model 3 
how the experimentally varied SQ water quality level influences the utility of choosing a water quality improvement program. Specifically, 
we include a variable capturing the current water quality score presented in each choice task, and we incorporate basin and sub-basin-level 
fixed effects to control for both observable SQ differences and unobserved basin-specific factors. This specification allows us to isolate 
how public preferences respond to exogenous variation in SQ, thereby mitigating endogeneity bias associated with non-random spatial variation 
in water quality conditions.

The statistically significant positive coefficients for the experimentally varied SQ water quality level indicate that as the 
experimentally induced SQ worsens, the utility derived from improving water quality increases. This suggests that respondents place a higher 
value on improvements when the experimentally manipulated baseline conditions are poorer. For example, the total WTP for a one unit 
improvement in water quality at the Upper South Saskatchewan sub basin, which is one of the sub basins within the South Saskatchewan 
River Basin, is estimated to be about \$635 when the SQ water quality is at level 5, whereas it is only about \$417 for the same 
improvement when the SQ is at level 2 (see Figure \ref{baseline_WTP}).


The results indicate a pronounced framing effect, whereby respondents anchor their preferences to the SQ water quality level. By 
leveraging experimentally induced variation along with basin- and sub-basin-level fixed effects, we isolate the influence of SQ conditions 
from unobserved spatial confounders, yielding more credible estimates than those based solely on observational differences. Importantly, 
the evidence shows that the way researchers define the SQ water quality level can meaningfully shape elicited preferences. Thus, the 
choice of SQ framing in stated preference surveys is not merely a design detail; it directly influences estimated willingness to pay for 
water quality improvements. Careful attention to baseline conditions is therefore essential for producing reliable and policy-relevant 
valuation evidence.

\subsubsection*{3.6.3. Influence of Spatial Attributes and Recreational Behavior}

In Table \ref{Table_4}, we present estimates from models that include additional covariates to examine variation in preferences for 
spatial attributes and respondents’ recreational behavior. Model 4 evaluates whether respondents place an additional premium on 
non-local choices that are adjacent to their local basin or sub-basin. To do so, we include variables that indicate whether water 
quality improvements in non-local basins or sub-basins are adjacent to the respondent’s local basin or sub-basin. The results 
indicate that individuals derive higher marginal utility from water quality improvements in non-local basins that are adjacent 
to their local basin, compared to non-local basins that are farther away. For instance, there is approximately a 35\% additional 
premium on the marginal WTP for non-local basin-level improvements that are adjacent to the respondent’s local basin (\$169), 
relative to similar improvements in non-local basins that are not adjacent (\$126).


As freshwater management policies and strategies vary across provinces, in Model 5, we assess how public preferences are shaped by provincial 
boundaries. Specifically, we use a continuous variable representing the percentage of the policy area within the respondent’s home province. 
The positive coefficient for this variable suggests that, as the proportion of the policy area within the respondent’s home province increases, 
the utility of the policy alternative also increases and, this relationship is statistically significant.

Model 6 assesses how respondents' policy preferences vary based on their recreational behavior. We include a variable indicating whether a 
respondent made a water-based recreational trip in 2024 to the same basin or sub-basin that is the focus of their choice scenarios. The positive 
and statistically significant coefficient suggests that respondents who took recreational trips within the policy-relevant basin or sub-basin 
derive significantly higher utility from the policy alternatives, reflecting greater use values compared to respondents who did not visit water 
bodies within the policy areas.

\subsubsection*{3.6.4. Evaluation of Response Validity and Robustness}
%\begin{onehalfspace}
    


To evaluate the validity, reliability, and respondent engagement of our stated preference survey, we incorporated several 
follow-up questions. Responses to questions assessing beliefs related to incentive-compatible elicitation revealed robust 
and valid preferences. Specifically, 81\% of participants reported voting as if their household would incur the stated policy 
cost, 80\% believed the proposed policies would achieve the stated water quality improvements, and 78\% responded as if the 
survey results would influence policymakers. The attributes of the water quality improvement program were salient to 
respondents, with 91\% indicating that their votes were influenced by the level of water quality improvement, 70\% by the 
spatial extent of the policy’s impact, and 70\% by improvements to water quality near their residence. The survey 
demonstrated strong perceived neutrality, with 78\% of respondents reporting that it allowed them to form their own 
opinions and 85\% expressing confidence in their votes, comparable to voting in a public election.

\subsection*{3.7. Discussion and Conclusion}
\addcontentsline{toc}{subsection}{\protect\numberline{3.7.}Discussion and Conclusion}





In SP surveys, particularly those valuing the economic benefits of water quality improvements, defining the SQ water quality is often 
challenging due to limited data. Consequently, researchers frequently need to make arbitrary decisions about how to frame SQ water 
quality levels. We examine whether these decisions on arbitrarily defined SQ levels influence public preferences, using a split-sample 
approach to experimentally manipulate SQ conditions. By leveraging this exogenous variation, we assess how the SQ affects public 
preferences for water quality improvements. Our use of experimentally induced variation allows for a more robust causal identification 
of the SQ's effects on public preferences, overcoming concerns associated with confounding from observational variability in the SQ. We 
find that researchers’ decisions regarding how the SQ is defined are not merely a technical detail of survey design—they meaningfully 
shape public preferences for water quality improvements. In particular, people derive higher utility from proposed improvements 
when baseline water quality is lower, whereas the same improvements are valued less when the SQ is relatively high. Ignoring 
such perceptual and framing biases when designing SP surveys may misrepresent the true societal value of environmental improvements. 
Therefore, the careful definition and communication of SQ conditions are critical for producing credible, policy-relevant valuation evidence.

Our empirical findings on public preferences for water quality improvements that inform policy, emphasizing the roles of 
spatial scale, location, and status quo conditions in determining WTP. Notably, spatial scale drives substantial preference 
variations, with marginal WTP estimated at local basins and sub-basins, is estimated at \$226, dropping to \$156 for 
non-local basins. In contrast, sub-basin-level improvements are estimated at \$147 locally versus \$86 non-locally. The total WTP for 
improving the water quality from each sub-basin’s current baseline to “good” (Level 2) at the sub-basin scale, considering 
only local values, ranges from \$320 to \$998, with higher WTP corresponding to areas with lower baseline health scores in our study region.
WTP patterns across spatial scales and local versus non-local contexts are consistent with the findings of \textcite{vossler2023valuing}. 
Although their spatial scales and water quality scales differ, at their smallest scale, they estimate a marginal WTP of \$121 
(2021 USD) for a one-unit increase in their six-level water quality score in a local sub-watershed. At the watershed scale, 
the marginal WTP for a local watershed is \$164, while it is lower, at \$135 (2021 USD), for a non-local watershed.


There are several limitations of this study. One limitation is arbitrary assignment of water quality values to sub-basins with missing data. 
Despite employing two scenarios to address uncertainty, this data imputation may affect the observed systematic variation in WTP across baseline conditions. 
This approach, particularly relevant in Canada’s data-scarce context, warrants careful scrutiny in future research to ensure robust valuation. 
Another limitation is the reliance on an online panel sample, which may underrepresent 
non-internet users, provide insufficient observations for severely degraded subsets, and depend on self-reported recreational behavior, potentially 
introducing bias. To address this, we applied weights to survey responses to align with 2021 Census population targets, mitigating bias from 
underrepresented groups.
